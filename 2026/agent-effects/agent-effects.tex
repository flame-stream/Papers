\title{External Effect Anomalies in AI Agent Workflows}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
AI agents act on the outside world through tools: they send messages, charge
cards, and place orders. Once externalized, such effects generally cannot be rolled back by the
runtime, only compensated, and agents release them while retrying failed calls, exploring
speculative branches, and sharing resources with other executions. The
mechanisms agent runtimes use for this --- replay, compensation, staged
release, coordinated access --- come from long-running and multilevel
transaction processing. What is new is the boundary: those models reason
over operations whose semantic properties are known, whereas an agent
typically calls third-party tools that declare little about themselves.

We give an effect-history model for this setting, separating what
externalized in the world from what the runtime observed about it, and a
catalog of eight external-effect anomalies grouped by the three ways
transactional control is lost: uncertainty about outcomes, the structure of
a single execution, and interaction with other executions and the open
world. Reading the catalog in the other direction yields the boundary
capabilities a runtime must establish to exclude each anomaly, together with
four boundaries above which no runtime provides a guarantee without
tool-side support. Current agent runtimes cover parts of the catalog by
supplying these capabilities themselves, out of band, which their own
adapter layers allow. Where tools instead arrive through a shared
interface, that option disappears. A census of 90{,}818 tools across
4{,}584 servers in the Model Context Protocol registry finds annotation
fields carried at scale but expressing little: three value signatures cover
77\% of tools, only 13.2\% carry an applicable destructiveness
classification, and none of the transactional capabilities beyond a bare
idempotence hint can be established from the standard annotation
vocabulary.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI agents increasingly take actions that change the outside world: sending
emails, charging cards, placing orders, calling external services. Such
effects often cannot be undone. Take an agent booking a trip on a 1500 EUR
budget. To save time it books the flight (900) and the hotel (700) in
parallel. Both calls succeed, but together they break the budget. The hotel
can be canceled; the flight was non-refundable. Every tool call worked, yet
the workflow left a state in the world that cannot be fully repaired.

Agents make this common because they run steps in parallel (supervisor-worker
decomposition), plan speculatively (tree search, best-of-$k$), execute likely
actions early to hide latency, and recover by retrying after failures. Each
is useful, but each can also produce a new kind of external-effect failure.
We argue these failures form one catalog, with three sources.
\emph{Uncertainty} failures: when the system cannot confirm the outcome of
an effect --- because of a crash, a timeout, or a lost acknowledgment --- and
must act anyway, the same effect may happen
twice, fail to happen while the workflow proceeds as if it had, or be
compensated blindly. \emph{Workflow} failures: an effect may outlive an
aborted workflow, leak before the workflow decides, or be depended on by a
committed branch though it came from an abandoned one. \emph{Interaction}
failures: an effect meets something outside its own execution --- a
concurrent execution writing the same resource, or an outside actor that
observes and reacts to it before it can be undone. Agents show all three,
because an agent is a long-running workflow that acts on tools whose
outcomes it cannot always confirm and shares an open world with others.

We define correctness through a catalog of anomalies, each a forbidden
pattern in the effect history. The intuition behind the catalog: a safe
execution is one whose surviving external effects can still be explained
as the intended committed workflow. Three features shape the setting: there is no rollback (an external
effect can only be compensated, and compensation is optional and may fail);
effects may be released before the workflow decides; and the surrounding world
is open and may react.

The mechanisms agent runtimes use --- replay, retry, compensation, staged
release, coordinated access --- are not new: they come from long-running
and multilevel transaction processing. What is different is the boundary.
Those models reason over operations whose relevant semantic properties are
known to, assumed by, or supplied to the transaction manager; an agent
typically calls third-party tools whose properties the interface leaves
largely undeclared. Current runtimes therefore commonly supply the properties they need
themselves --- adapter annotations, registered inverses, per-effect tiers ---
out of band and for themselves alone. Better replay and recovery do not close
the gap on their own: the missing piece is a contract layer at the tool
interface.

This paper makes four contributions. First, an effect-history model for
agent workflows whose operations are calls to externally supplied tools
(Section~\ref{sec:model}). Second, a catalog of eight external-effect
anomalies, organized by the three ways transactional control is lost ---
uncertainty, workflow structure, and interaction --- with guarantee
profiles naming the exclusion sets (Section~\ref{sec:catalog}). Third, a
reading of current agent runtimes as partial coverage of the catalog
(Section~\ref{sec:systems}). Fourth, for each anomaly, the tool contracts
that make excluding it achievable, and four guarantee boundaries
identifying where runtime-only mechanisms are insufficient under our model
(Section~\ref{sec:contracts}), grounded by a registry-wide census of MCP
tool annotations (Section~\ref{sec:grounding}).

\section{Effect Histories and the Tool Boundary}
\label{sec:model}

Agent systems form a hierarchy of levels, in the spirit of multilevel
transaction management, where the guarantees of one level are derived from
the properties of the operations below it~\cite{weikum1991principles}. We
work with a slice of two adjacent levels $L_i$ and $L_{i+1}$, written L0
and L1 for readability. L0 is the level of operations the agent observes
as atomic calls --- in current deployments, typically tools behind external
APIs: \texttt{charge()}, \texttt{send\_email()}, \texttt{book\_flight()},
\texttt{publish\_post()}. L1 is the level of workflows composed from these
calls: \texttt{HireCandidate}, \texttt{BookTrip},
\texttt{NegotiateContract}. A workflow turns individual tool calls into a
logical unit of work, and can itself serve as an operation one level up.
The history is defined at L0 over external events and the runtime's
observations of them; the runtime generally sees only a projection of it.
Effect safety is defined at L1, against the workflow's decisions.

The slice is relative in the other direction too. An L0 operation
is atomic only from the caller's side: for its provider,
\texttt{book\_flight} is a workflow over operations the caller does not
see, and neither party knows how deep the chain goes --- the booking system
may involve further agents of its own. When that hidden structure shows
through, for example as a timeout in the middle of the provider's workflow,
the caller is left with an outcome it cannot confirm.

The boundary also varies in who owns it. When agent and tools are developed
together, the properties of L0 operations can be declared by construction,
and the setting is close to a classical multilevel system. A runtime that
owns its adapter layer is in a similar position: it can attach the
properties it needs to each tool itself, tool by tool. Neither option is
available once tools arrive through a shared interface: the agent then
learns about an operation only what that interface carries, and adding a
property means changing the interface for everyone. The Model Context
Protocol (MCP) is the widely used interface of this kind, and it is
publicly inspectable, which is why we return to it when we ask what today's
boundary actually declares (Section~\ref{sec:grounding}). Whichever way
the properties are obtained, they constrain which effect-safety guarantees
are achievable for the composed L1 workflow.

We focus on the following properties of L0 operations:

\begin{itemize}
\item idempotence,
\item invertibility,
\item externalization,
\item determinism,
\item commutativity.
\end{itemize}

These properties are independent: \texttt{increment} commutes but is not
idempotent, while \texttt{delete} is idempotent but does not commute. They
therefore form a multidimensional property space rather than a single scale
(e.g.\ a reversible/irreversible axis). Commutativity is moreover \emph{relational}
(a property of operation pairs) and often \emph{conditional} on state
(escrow-style~\cite{oneil1986escrow}); determinism, not usually treated as
an operation contract in classical concurrency control, is an additional
axis --- black-box LLM-backed tools can be nondeterministic.

We model execution as histories over two layers: what happened outside, and
what the runtime learned about it. The outer world is described by:

\begin{itemize}
\item $attempt(a, \ell)$: the runtime issues attempt $a$ of logical
operation $\ell$. Several attempts may carry the same $\ell$ --- a retry is
a second attempt of the same logical operation,
\item $externalize(q, e)$: a runtime-issued action $q$ --- either an
operation attempt or a compensating action, both introduced below ---
produced external effect $e$, the moment it becomes visible outside the
runtime and is no longer under full transactional control. When it matters,
we write $externalize(q, e \text{ on } r)$ to name the external resource
$r$,
\item $cmp(c, a)$: the runtime issues compensating action $c$ for attempt
$a$, and $neutralizes(c, e)$ when $c$ successfully neutralizes an effect
$e$ of $a$. A compensating action externalizes in its own right, so it may
appear as $externalize(c, x)$,
\item $externalize_{\mathit{ext}}(p, x)$: an external party $p$ produces effect $x$
outside the runtime's control --- no attempt of the runtime precedes it and
no compensation of the runtime reaches it,
\item $dep(e_2 \leftarrow e_1)$: $e_2$ causally depends on $e_1$ (it was
produced by a party that had observed $e_1$),
\item $commit(w)$, $abort(w)$.
\end{itemize}

What the runtime knows is separate:

\begin{itemize}
\item $observe(a, s)$ with $s \in \{\mathit{confirmed}, \mathit{failed},
\mathit{unknown}\}$: the outcome the runtime obtained for attempt $a$.
\emph{Unknown} means no acknowledgment was received; whether
$externalize(a, e)$ happened is then not determined by the runtime's
observations, though it is determined in the world.
\end{itemize}

Finally, a workflow $w$ has a set $Req(w)$ of logical operations its commit
asserts to have taken effect, and we define:

\begin{itemize}
\item $resolved(w)$: a $commit(w)$ or $abort(w)$ event has occurred, i.e.\
the workflow's outcome is decided and will not change,
\item $survives(e)$: $e$ was externalized and no later compensation
neutralized it --- the effect remains in the external world.
\end{itemize}

Keeping the two layers apart matters for the anomalies below: an effect
either occurred or did not, while \emph{unknown} is a state of the runtime,
and acting under it is what several of the anomalies turn on. We write
$effect(\ell)$ and $cmp(\ell)$ for the externalization and compensation of a
logical operation when the individual attempt is not at issue.

Unlike classical read-write histories~\cite{adya1999},
effect histories describe what the workflow externalized to the
outside world. The interesting anomalies live in the window between
externalization and $resolved$/$cmp$ --- a window absent from read-write
models.

\section{Effect Anomalies}
\label{sec:catalog}

Each anomaly exposes something missing at the agent--tool boundary:
evidence (A1--A3), lifecycle control (A4--A6), or coordination (A7--A8).
Some describe an incorrect surviving history; others are profile-relative
patterns that a stronger guarantee excludes preventively, because relying
on a favourable outcome is itself unsafe. We state each in
one sentence and then give a formal trace as an illustrative example; the
trace is one way the anomaly can arise, not its definition. We tag each
anomaly by its \emph{source}: \emph{uncertainty} (A1--A3), \emph{workflow}
(A4--A6), or \emph{interaction} (A7--A8). The uncertainty anomalies arise when
the system cannot confirm the outcome of an effect and must act anyway; the
unconfirmed outcome can come from a crash, a timeout, or a lost or absent
acknowledgment. The workflow anomalies arise from the structure of a
single execution --- survival under abort, dependency, and timing relative to
its resolution. The interaction anomalies arise when an effect meets
something outside its own execution: another execution writing the same
resource (A7), or an external actor that observes and reacts to it (A8).
The other party need not be human: it may itself be an agent workflow,
running at a level our boundary does not see.

\paragraph{A1: Duplicated Effect (source: uncertainty)}
\emph{Two attempts of the same logical operation both externalize.} This
arises when the outcome of a call is not confirmed, so the runtime
re-issues it, and both attempts take effect. For example:
\begin{verbatim}
attempt(a1, pay_invoice)
externalize(a1, e1)            the payment goes through
observe(a1, unknown)           no acknowledgment (timeout)
attempt(a2, pay_invoice)       re-issued, assuming a1 did not
externalize(a2, e2)
=> invoice paid twice
\end{verbatim}
Non-determinism makes this worse: re-issuing a non-deterministic operation
yields not a duplicate but a \emph{different} effect. Idempotence neutralizes
repetition; determinism does not by itself make re-issue safe, but it makes
replay matching meaningful, since a re-issued call then denotes the same
effect.

\paragraph{A2: Missing Committed Effect (source: uncertainty)}
\emph{A workflow commits relying on an effect that did not successfully
externalize --- either because an unknown outcome was resolved optimistically
or because a failed or absent required effect was ignored.} The canonical
case is uncertainty resolved the wrong way: the runtime assumes an
unconfirmed effect succeeded and commits, though nothing externalized. For
example, with $pay\_invoice \in Req(Order)$:
\begin{verbatim}
attempt(a1, pay_invoice)
observe(a1, unknown)           outcome not confirmed
commit(Order)                  assumes success; nothing did
=> required effect absent, yet the workflow committed
\end{verbatim}
The anomaly is narrow: it is \emph{not} that the agent omitted a step
(a planning failure), but that a required effect is absent from the
committed history --- whether its outcome was unknown and guessed wrong, or
known to have failed and committed over anyway. A2 belongs to the
uncertainty family because its characteristic mechanism is commit without
authoritative evidence that the required effect externalized; the
known-failure case is the degenerate form in which such evidence is
available but ignored.

\paragraph{A3: Orphaned Compensation (source: uncertainty)}
\emph{A compensation is issued for an attempt whose outcome is unknown.}
This arises when an unconfirmed outcome is instead resolved
pessimistically: the runtime compensates without knowing whether the
original attempt externalized. For example:
\begin{verbatim}
attempt(a1, pay_invoice)
observe(a1, unknown)           outcome not confirmed
cmp(c1, a1)                    compensation issued blindly
externalize(c1, refund)        the refund itself takes effect
=> if a1 never externalized, the refund is spurious
\end{verbatim}
The anomaly is the \emph{blind action} at the moment of $cmp$, not the
eventual outcome; the remedy is to resolve $unknown$ before compensating.
A1, A2, and A3 are a family: all three stem from an outcome the system
cannot confirm, and differ only in the reaction. Faced with an unconfirmed
outcome, the runtime may re-issue (and duplicate, A1), assume success and
commit (and miss a required effect, A2), or compensate blindly (A3).
Resolving the outcome before acting removes all three. A1 and A2 are
visible in the final history: a duplicate is there, a required effect is
not. A3 is different: compensating an unknown outcome is wrong at the
moment it is done, whatever the outcome later turns out to be. The family
also shows where this setting differs from classical transaction
processing. There, an in-doubt outcome is temporary: participants run a
recovery protocol and resolve it. A third-party tool runs no such protocol,
so an unknown outcome stays unknown until the tool itself offers a way to
resolve it --- a durable acknowledgment, a status endpoint, or idempotent
re-issue. When agent and tools are developed together, the classical
picture applies; the family concerns the third-party boundary.

\paragraph{A4: Uncompensated Residue (source: workflow)}
\emph{An aborted workflow leaves behind a surviving effect that was not
successfully neutralized} --- because no compensation exists, it was not
invoked, it failed, or it was incomplete. For example, a non-refundable
ticket is purchased, then the trip is aborted and the purchase cannot be
undone:
\begin{verbatim}
effect(book_flight)                non-refundable
abort(BookTrip)                    budget violated
cmp(book_flight)                   fails: no refund path
=> survives(book_flight): cannot retract; money lost
\end{verbatim}

\paragraph{A5: Premature Externalization (source: workflow)}
\emph{An effect that may not survive is externalized before its workflow is
resolved.} For example, an offer letter is sent before legal approval
completes, and approval is later denied:
\begin{verbatim}
effect(send_offer)                 letter externalized
NOT resolved(Hire)                 not yet committed/aborted
abort(Hire)                        approval later denied
=> effect(send_offer) preceded resolved(Hire)
\end{verbatim}
A5 is profile-relative: it is not a violation under compensation-safe
execution, which may externalize an effect early and compensate later; it
is the forbidden pattern that speculation-safe execution excludes by
staging or gating effects that may not survive until the workflow's
resolution is known.

\paragraph{A6: Contaminated Speculation (source: workflow)}
\emph{A committed effect causally depends on an effect from a branch that
does not survive.} For example, a committed branch externalizes an action
based on a temporary reservation made by another branch that is later
canceled:
\begin{verbatim}
effect(reserve_room R) [b1]        room R held
dep(send_invites <- reserve_room R) [b2] b2 needs R
effect(send_invites) [b2]          invites naming R sent
commit(b2)                               b2 wins, committed
abort(b1); cmp(reserve_room R)           b1 loses; R released
=> b2 already announced R, which no longer survives
\end{verbatim}
The contamination is causal, not about reversibility: $b2$ trusted an
effect of an unresolved branch. This is the effect-world analogue of a
\emph{dirty read} --- reading uncommitted state that is later rolled
back --- except the ``read'' is a causal dependency on an external effect.
The harm (invitations already sent) would arise even if those invitations
were themselves reversible.

\paragraph{A7: Conflicting Externalization (source: interaction)}
\emph{Two independent executions release potentially non-commuting effects
on a shared resource without an ordering or mediation contract available to
the runtime, so the outcome may depend on their interleaving.} For example, two coding workers push to the same branch
from the same base:
\begin{verbatim}
effect(push X on main) [w1]        w1 pushes from base
effect(push Y on main) [w2]        w2 from the same base
NOT dep(push Y <- push X)          neither saw the other
NOT commute(push X, push Y)        order changes the result
=> the effects race; outcome depends on interleaving
\end{verbatim}
Unlike contaminated speculation (A6), there is no dependency between the two
executions --- neither observed the other; both acted on the shared resource
independently. Commutativity \emph{excludes} the anomaly: if the two effects
commute, order is irrelevant. Invertibility does not exclude it but makes it
\emph{repairable}: a versioned reversible resource lets the loser be rolled
back and re-applied toward a serializable final state, as in classical
concurrency control. Mediation prevents the race by ordering access. The
conflict is beyond runtime repair when the effects are both non-commuting
and irreversible and no mediator prevented their unordered externalization.
A7 is profile-relative, as A5 is: it is the pattern excluded by
externally-mediated execution, in which concurrent executions do not
release potentially non-commuting effects on a shared resource without an
ordering or mediation contract. The resource may serialize the calls on its
own, and the resulting state may then admit a serial explanation; the
stronger profile forbids relying on that outcome, since nothing at the
boundary establishes it.

\paragraph{A8: Phantom Compensation (source: interaction, open-world)}
\emph{An effect is compensated, but an external consequence that depends on
it survives.} For example, a supplier reacts to an offer that is later
withdrawn:
\begin{verbatim}
externalize(a1, e)          offer sent; supplier sees it
externalize_ext(S, x)       supplier acts on the offer
dep(x <- e)                 x produced having observed e
cmp(c1, a1)                 offer withdrawn
neutralizes(c1, e)          the offer effect is undone
survives(x)                 the supplier's action stands
=> the offer is retracted; the reaction to it is not
\end{verbatim}
The dependent effect $x$ is exogenous: no attempt of the runtime produced
it and no compensation of the runtime reaches it. This
separates A8 from A6, where a surviving committed effect depends on a
managed effect that did not survive; here the surviving dependent effect is
itself unmanaged. Unlike A7, the reacting party may sit outside any
contractual boundary, so no ordering or commutativity can be declared for
it; the enforceable primitive is preventive --- control whether and when the
effect becomes externally observable. A8 also
stands apart from A1--A7. Those concern effects within the managed or
mediated boundary, so they can be detected and sometimes repaired after the
fact. An exogenous consequence lies outside the projection the runtime
observes and can compensate: the actor saw the effect and acted, and that
action and its consequences are beyond what the runtime records or
controls. Compensation can retract the effect but not the
reaction to it, which is why A8 is irremediable when the reacting party is
outside the mediated boundary (see the externally-mediated guarantee below).

\subsection{Safety Guarantee Profiles}
\label{sec:levels}

The anomaly catalog induces a vocabulary of \emph{effect-safety guarantees}:
each guarantee names a set of anomalies it excludes.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unknown-safe} (excludes A1, A2, A3): the system never
duplicates, loses a required committed effect, or blindly compensates an
effect whose outcome it cannot confirm.
\item \textbf{Compensation-safe} (excludes A4): an aborted workflow leaves
no uncompensated residue, assuming compensations are correct, complete, and
successful.
\item \textbf{Speculation-safe} (excludes A5, A6): no effect that may not
survive is externalized before the workflow is resolved, and no committed
branch depends on an unresolved one.
\item \textbf{Externally-mediated} (excludes A7, A8): interactions with
other executions and outside actors are mediated --- shared-resource writes
are ordered or made to commute, and external reactions to intermediate
effects are controlled where the boundary admits such mediation.
\end{itemize}

The catalog reflects how external the cause of an anomaly is. A1--A6 arise
within a single managed execution and can in principle be prevented through
deduplication, delivery guarantees, outcome resolution, gating, dependency
tracking, and branch isolation. A7 and A8 arise from interaction beyond the execution: a
concurrent execution writing a shared resource (A7), or an outside actor
that observes and reacts to an effect before it is compensated (A8).

Interactive tasks explain why the compensation-safe regime is sometimes the
best achievable one. Consider an agent negotiating on a user's behalf. It
cannot learn the counterparty's reaction without sending an offer, and
sending an offer is already an effect: the action must precede the
observation. Full gating is therefore not available. The best attainable
guarantee allows the workflow to externalize effects sequentially while
avoiding uncompensated residue in discarded branches. When the inputs needed
to resolve the workflow can be observed without effect, for example through
a \texttt{quote} operation, the stronger gated guarantee becomes attainable.

\subsection{Scope and Coverage}
\label{sec:coverage}

The catalog is coverage-oriented rather than complete by theorem: it covers
the ways transactional control over external effects is lost within our
model --- absolute completeness is unavailable in an open world, where an
external actor can react in unbounded ways. Each family has a structural
reason for its membership. The uncertainty
family is fixed by the reactions available when the outcome of a required
logical operation is not confirmed: re-issue (A1), commit as though it had
externalized (A2), or compensate (A3). The workflow family is fixed by how
an externalized effect of a single execution relates to its resolution
point --- through survival under abort (A4), timing (A5), and dependency
(A6), the three internal relations considered in our model. The interaction
family separates unmediated concurrent effects on a shared resource (A7)
from an exogenous consequence of a later-compensated effect (A8); the other
combinations reduce to A5/A6, to a stale read, or to information disclosure.

This structure suggests how completeness relative to the model might be
argued: rather than enumerate every possible failure, one would state the
positive lifecycle conditions these families describe and show that a history
satisfying them exhibits none of A1--A8, and conversely. Stating those conditions independently of the anomaly names is
what would keep the equivalence from being a tautology; delimiting the
relation families it rests on (adding authorization, read consistency, or
deadlines would introduce more) is part of the same problem. We conjecture that,
relative to the event and relation vocabulary of Section~\ref{sec:model},
every loss of transactional control the model can represent falls into one
of the three families above. Formal coverage, minimality, and independence
are left as future work.

We explicitly place the following outside the model, so the catalog is not
mistaken for covering them: semantic planning errors (the agent booked the
wrong city); read-side anomalies (stale or inconsistent reads --- the
subject of read-side isolation work such as S-Bus); security and policy
violations (a secret leaked to a channel); tool-contract misclassification
(a tool declared reversible that is not); the order in which an execution's
own repeated or independent effects are externalized (which a fuller
treatment would add as an uncertainty condition, but which we leave aside
here) --- except where order becomes observable as a shared-resource
interaction between executions, which the catalog does capture, as A7; and
liveness failures (a workflow that never finishes). These may matter, but
they are not external-effect anomalies in the sense studied here.

\section{Existing Systems as Partial Coverage}
\label{sec:systems}

Existing systems can be placed against the guarantee profiles of
Section~\ref{sec:levels} by the coverage they target under their stated
assumptions: none of them realizes a clean cumulative hierarchy, so
Table~\ref{tab:systems} reports the set of anomalies each one addresses.

\textbf{System} & \textbf{Guarantees (anomalies)} & \textbf{Notes} \\

The coverage entries should be read as the guarantees a system targets under
its stated assumptions, not as proven results. ACRFence targets A1 for the
nondeterministic-replay case: after checkpoint-restore an LLM re-synthesizes
a syntactically different but semantically equivalent irreversible call,
which servers accept as new; ACRFence proposes logging effects and enforcing
replay-or-fork, validating the attack but not yet implementing the
mitigation. RAC targets A4 by compensation rather than gating: effects
externalize \emph{immediately}, and the runtime logs them and runs
compensations on abort in dependency order (dependents before parents).
Because it does not gate, an irreversible effect that has externalized
before an abort can leave residue, and when a compensator is missing RAC
assumes the tool had no side effect; its A4 coverage is therefore partial.
It also has no idempotency mechanism, so the unknown-outcome family (A1--A3)
is not its target.

Atomix and Cordon instead \emph{gate} irreversible
effects until commit, which removes A4 for gated irreversible effects; for
eagerly externalized reversible effects, A4 is excluded under the usual
compensation assumptions. They
avoid A1 and A3 only on paths where unknown outcomes are not blindly
compensated: either the effect is gated and has not externalized, or
re-issue is safe through idempotent, known-outcome tool semantics. This is
not a general resolve-before-compensate protocol --- without idempotency,
status resolution, or durable acknowledgment, A1/A3 fall outside their
guarantee, which is why we mark them partial.

Their remaining gap is also at
the moment of release: several gated irreversibles cannot be externalized
atomically, so a crash mid-release can commit while one required effect is
missing --- an instance of A2, not A4, which is why A2 is absent from their
coverage. Atomix
adds frontier-gated dependency tracking under speculation and contention
(A6), and its frontiers also order concurrent writes to a shared resource,
which partially addresses conflicting externalization (A7) --- but ordering
is not atomicity, so non-commuting irreversible effects can still race;
for Cordon, A7 is outside its task-scoped boundary rather than a guarantee it
targets.

CoAgent addresses A7 directly: it fixes a serialization order at
launch, applies writes speculatively, and mechanically undoes and reorders
misplaced writes through a saga-style inverse each tool registers in advance,
reaching serializability at quiescence. Its two mechanisms are exactly the
gates our catalog predicts --- it reorders when operations commute and inverts
when they do not --- and it hits the same limit: an irreversible effect can be
neither reordered nor inverted, so CoAgent falls back to gating it (its calls
are refused until predecessors commit). It thus realizes externally-mediated
safety only under a registered-inverse (or commutativity) contract, an
instance of the achievability condition for A7 rather than an unconditional
guarantee.

Shepherd~\cite{yu2026shepherd} approaches the same boundary as a
substrate rather than a runtime guarantee: every effect in its execution
trace carries a reversibility tier --- reversible, compensable, or
irreversible --- matching the classes our catalog builds on; reversible and
compensable effects can be rolled back, while irreversible effects
materialize on emission and are recorded for audit. Its supervisor use case
prevents conflicts among parallel coding agents on a shared repository ---
an observed instance of A7, handled as coordination rather than named as an
anomaly. None of the surveyed systems controls an outside actor's reaction to a
released effect, so phantom compensation (A8) is uncovered.

Across these systems a common pattern stands out. Each one needs the same
properties of tool operations --- idempotency, reversibility class, a
compensator, commutativity --- and each obtains them out of band: Atomix
through adapter-declared keys and effect classes, Cordon through its own
reversibility annotations, CoAgent through registered inverses, Shepherd
through per-effect tiers. In these systems the declarations are not
obtained from a shared, standardized agent-facing contract, and are
therefore not reusable across runtimes. No single system covers the full
catalog.

\section[Transactional Tool Contracts and Guarantee Boundaries]{Transactional Tool Contracts and\\ Guarantee Boundaries}
\label{sec:contracts}

The pattern at the end of Section~\ref{sec:systems} is the starting point.
Each runtime obtains the properties it needs out of band: common
agent-facing tool interfaces expose at most advisory hints of these
properties. These declarations are not an alternative
to runtime protocols but their precondition: a protocol is defined relative
to what the tool declares. This section specifies that layer --- the
transactional contracts a tool interface should expose --- and the
boundaries where no runtime provides a guarantee without tool-side support.
Protocols parameterized by these contracts are the natural next step,
outside the scope of this paper.

\subsection{Contract Gaps}
\label{sec:unknown}

The model takes operation properties --- invertibility, idempotence,
commutativity --- as known, as advanced transaction models do. For
LLM-driven agents this is the assumption most often violated: tools arrive
as black boxes, and the agent infers or guesses whether an effect is
compensable, idempotent, or commutative, often incorrectly (recall RAC's
assumption that a tool without a compensator has no side effect). The gap
is not always the
provider's choice: the operation behind a tool call may itself be a
workflow of unknown depth on the provider's side, so the provider may not
know the end-to-end property either.

Read in the other direction, the catalog specifies the boundary evidence,
control, and coordination a runtime needs in order to exclude each anomaly
generally. When the tool interface does not expose these primitives, a
runtime synthesizes substitutes through adapters, annotations, assumptions,
or external coordination. A contract layer should also record what
is \emph{not} known: a property may be known, assumed, or unknown, and an
action taken under a mistaken assumption --- compensating an operation that
has no true inverse, replaying one wrongly believed idempotent --- is a
failure mode of its own.

The systems of Section~\ref{sec:systems} can synthesize such substitutes
because each owns its adapter layer. A runtime consuming tools through a
shared interface reads what that interface carries, and MCP is not
entirely silent: since its 2025-03-26 revision, tool annotations let a
server flag a tool as read-only, destructive, idempotent, or
open-world.\footnote{Model Context Protocol specification, revision
2025-03-26.} These are advisory boolean hints --- unenforced, optional, and
coarser than the capabilities below: \texttt{idempotentHint} marks a
property but supplies no idempotency key, and no hint covers status
resolution, compensation, staging, commutativity, or visibility. What
servers do with them is measured in Section~\ref{sec:grounding}. Because an agent's execution graph is generated as
it runs, the layer must be usable by a runtime as effects appear, not only
by static analysis of a fixed plan.

\subsection{Contracts per Anomaly}

Several primitives recur across the catalog. We consolidate them per
anomaly. Each row names a \emph{boundary capability} --- a semantic
property a generic runtime can establish from the agent-facing boundary ---
with representative realizations and the runtime mechanism it enables. The
rows identify capabilities, not unique interface designs: the listed
realizations are representative, and equivalent sources of evidence,
control, or coordination may satisfy the same capability. A capability is
what a runtime can establish from the exposed boundary; a
\emph{contract} is the reusable declaration or interface structure through
which it is exposed --- possibly composed of several operations and their
documentation. Without an equivalent source of evidence, control, or
coordination the corresponding guarantee is unavailable in the general
case, and protocols consume these capabilities to establish workflow
guarantees. Table~\ref{tab:contracts} lists them per anomaly.

The uncertainty family needs an authoritative way to converge on one
logical outcome: status resolution, a durable acknowledgment, or idempotent
re-issue that returns the authoritative outcome of the original logical
operation. A key that merely deduplicates is weaker: it excludes A1 but
leaves the runtime without the outcome that A2 and A3 turn on. Among the
systems of Section~\ref{sec:systems}, the adapter-declared idempotency key
is the closest primitive in use. Atomic-batch
participation --- a tool-side prepare/commit point after which a batch of
effects either all persists or none does --- is supplied by none of the
systems we survey, and A2 on multi-effect release remains uncovered.

A4 turns on the compensation declaration: the compensator's preconditions,
what counts as its success, and an explicit signal when none exists, rather
than a silent assumption that none is needed. In practice tools arrive as
bare API calls in the systems surveyed, so whether a compensator can be
\emph{synthesized} --- from
the signature, the description, and the observed effect --- gates how far
compensation-safety reaches. A synthesized compensator has uncertain
semantics of its own, and for some operations only a partial or costly
compensation exists, if any.

The gated guarantee against premature externalization (A5) needs the inputs
of a workflow invariant to be observable without an effect. In
\texttt{BookTrip}, the budget invariant depends on flight and hotel prices.
If prices are revealed only by booking, checking the invariant requires
externalizing --- a cycle: to check, book; to book safely, check --- and
gating is not achievable. A read-only \texttt{quote} exposes the prices
without effect and breaks the cycle. This condition --- the values an
invariant depends on can be observed before irreversible effects must occur
--- is necessary for gating, and in simple staged-effect models it is also
sufficient. A \texttt{hold} or \texttt{reserve} with an explicit expiry
stages the effect itself.

Dependency tracking (A6) needs stable effect identity, and the unit it
protects is a \emph{commit sphere}: a minimal set of effects that must
become final together. In \texttt{BookTrip} the charge depends on both
bookings and the confirmation email asserts all three; committing one
without the others leaves a payment without a booking, or a confirmation
that lies. The name is by analogy with persistence
spheres~\cite{weikum1991principles}, where a minimal set of pages must
reach disk together. Spheres are discovered as the graph grows, their
members differ in reversibility, and their size measures the cost of the
guarantee.

A7 and A8 need contracts at a different granularity. Idempotency, status,
quote, and compensation are properties of a single operation. Commutativity
is a property of a \emph{pair} of operations on a resource: it cannot be
set by rules given to one agent, so it must be declared at the resource ---
which operations commute, which have inverses, and, where neither holds, a
mediator that serializes access. Visibility is likewise a property of the
boundary: who can observe an effect before it is final. The systems of
Section~\ref{sec:systems} obtain neither from the boundary, and A8 remains
uncovered.

Read against the profiles of Section~\ref{sec:levels}: unknown-safe needs
an authoritative convergence primitive for the logical operation ---
durable outcome resolution, or idempotent re-issue returning the original
outcome --- with outcome-conditioned compensation addressing A3 once the
outcome is known; compensation-safe needs the A4 row; speculation-safe
needs the A5--A6 rows; external mediation needs the A7--A8 rows. A tool that exposes these capabilities makes the corresponding
exclusions implementable by any runtime, given a suitable protocol and
declarations that hold, in place of the out-of-band declarations of
Section~\ref{sec:systems}.

\subsection{Guarantee Boundaries Above Black-Box Tools}
\label{sec:boundaries}

Four boundaries mark where no runtime-side mechanism provides a guarantee
without tool-side support. We state them as consequences of the definitions,
instantiated by current systems; formal impossibility proofs are future
work.

First, without a primitive that lets retries converge on the authoritative
outcome --- no idempotent re-issue returning the original outcome, no status
endpoint, no durable acknowledgment --- no general unknown-safe execution is
available. Over an unreliable channel the runtime cannot
learn, in bounded time, whether an effect took place: the classical
exactly-once barrier~\cite{bernstein1987concurrency} at the agent--tool
boundary. The runtime can only choose which reaction to risk --- re-issue,
commit optimistically, or compensate --- trading A1 against A2 against A3.

Second, non-commuting irreversible effects without a mediator admit no
general conflict repair. Repair means restoring a chosen serial order, by
undoing and re-applying effects --- which irreversibility rules out. What
remains is prevention: mediation before unordered externalization, or
gating conflicting calls until earlier ones commit, which is what CoAgent
does.

Third, an open-world reaction ends the reach of compensation. Once an
outside actor has observed a released effect and acted, retracting the
effect does not retract the reaction (A8): no contract with the tool
restores a closed history. The available contract is preventive ---
visibility control before observation.

Fourth, several irreversible effects on different tools cannot be released
atomically above the tool layer. A crash between releases leaves some
effects placed and others missing (A2). Making the batch atomic is the
classical atomic-commitment problem~\cite{bernstein1987concurrency} and
needs tool-side prepare/commit participation. Atomix states this
limit for its own release step.

\section{What the Boundary Declares Today}
\label{sec:grounding}

Section~\ref{sec:systems} showed each runtime supplying the capabilities it
needs itself, out of band, which its own adapter layer allows;
Section~\ref{sec:contracts} specified the layer those capabilities would
form once tools arrive through a shared interface. What such an interface
carries today is an empirical question, and this section reports a census
of the standard annotations MCP defines. That the anomalies themselves occur is
documented by the systems of Section~\ref{sec:systems}: ACRFence validates
the duplicated-effect attack on real executions, and Shepherd's supervisor
is built around observed conflicts between parallel agents.

Two properties of the census bound what it can show. It measures declared
values on the agent-facing boundary, not the behavior of the service
behind it: a value states what a server asserts, and we do not verify it.
And it reports what a runtime can read from the boundary, so a capability
we do not find is reported as \emph{not established from the exposed
boundary}, not as absent from the underlying system.

\subsection{An Annotation Census of the MCP Registry}

We took a full snapshot of the official MCP registry on 2026-07-28
(59{,}625 entries; 18{,}688 distinct servers, keeping the latest version of
each) and queried every anonymously reachable remote server with
\texttt{tools/list} (15\,s timeout, no retries), against specification
revision 2025-03-26. No tool was
called. Of 7{,}987 such targets, 4{,}584 returned at least one tool,
yielding 90{,}818 tools (median 11 per server). The remainder failed to
connect or timed out; servers requiring authentication and stdio-only
packages are out of scope, so the sample covers anonymously reachable
remote integrations. For each tool we recorded the four annotations the
specification defines --- \texttt{readOnlyHint}, \texttt{destructiveHint},
\texttt{idempotentHint}, \texttt{openWorldHint} --- keeping an explicit
\texttt{false} distinct from an omitted field.

\paragraph{Annotations are widely transmitted.}
76.3\% of tools carry at least one annotation and 65.3\% carry all four.
Transmission is not the same as
semantic information, however: the four booleans are serialized whether or
not their values say anything about the particular tool. The specification
renders \texttt{destructiveHint} vacuous when \texttt{readOnlyHint} is
true, and that pairing alone covers 56\% of all tools.

\paragraph{The values that are sent are concentrated.}
Reading each tool as a signature over the four fields, 66 distinct
signatures appear, but their frequencies are highly concentrated: one
signature --- read-only, non-destructive, idempotent, open-world --- covers
42.7\% of tools, and three cover 76.7\%. Servers do differentiate: among multi-tool servers that annotate,
77.3\% apply more than one signature. The differentiation is coarse,
though: for the median server the
most common signature still covers 79\% of its tools. So while annotations
do separate tools, the effective annotation vocabulary a runtime
encounters is dominated by a few recurring patterns. The registry therefore shows that annotation
fields are already carried at scale; the limitation is not their presence
on the wire but the resolution of what they can say. We do not attribute
the values to author intent: many may originate in SDK defaults or server
templates.

\paragraph{What the hints do not resolve.}
The specification makes \texttt{destructiveHint} meaningful only when
\texttt{readOnlyHint} is false. The field is present on 69.3\% of tools,
but only 13.2\% are in a position to say anything about destructiveness at
all, and 3.0\% assert a destructive operation. On a name-derived subset of
effectful tools, about one tool in ten asserts
\texttt{destructiveHint} true; the rest omit the field or declare it
false.\footnote{The subset is a name
heuristic, kept independent of the annotation values to avoid
circularity; it agrees with \texttt{readOnlyHint} on about 91\% of tools
where both are available. We treat it as indicative.}

\noindent
The four hints describe properties of a single call: whether it reads,
whether repeating it adds nothing, whether it reaches outside.

Section~\ref{sec:contracts} against that vocabulary.

The gap is not confined to effectful tools --- read-only operations also
have properties the vocabulary cannot state, such as determinism or
freshness --- but these are the ones a transactional protocol consumes.

Current MCP metadata is thus sufficient to distinguish coarse execution
modes --- read against write, declared idempotent against not --- and
insufficient to express the semantics a runtime needs to reason about
retries, compensation, speculation, or concurrency. The ecosystem already
provides metadata at scale; what is missing is the vocabulary needed for
transactional reasoning.

\subsection{From Declarations to Implementations (TBD)}

The census measures what servers \emph{declare} at the boundary. It leaves
open a second question: what a runtime could establish if it read
everything the boundary makes public, including the documentation and the
behaviour of the underlying service. A tool that accepts a client-supplied
request identifier and offers a lookup by that identifier provides
authoritative convergence whether or not any annotation says so; a tool
whose upstream API takes an idempotency key but whose wrapper drops it
provides none, however the wrapper is annotated.

We plan a second study over MCP server implementations, using the published
dataset of MCP repositories on GitHub~\cite{toeppe2026mcpdataset} as a
sampling frame. For a stratified sample of servers we would extract the
registered tools together with the upstream calls they make, and code each
effectful operation against the capabilities of
Section~\ref{sec:contracts}. Coding follows the same convention as above:
a capability is recorded as established only on positive evidence ---
machine-readable in the interface, or documented --- and otherwise as not
established from what the boundary publishes. Two cases are worth
separating: a capability that no party provides, and one the upstream API
provides but the tool wrapper does not carry, which locates the loss at the
integration layer rather than at the service.

Aggregating per anomaly and then per guarantee profile would answer the
question this paper leaves open: not whether the vocabulary is expressive
enough, which Section~\ref{sec:grounding} already indicates, but how often
the guarantees of Section~\ref{sec:levels} are within reach of a runtime
over tools as they are published today. \emph{[Study to be carried out;
numbers to be added.]}

\section{Related Work}

Classical transaction models introduced isolation levels and anomaly
definitions~\cite{berenson1995critique,adya1999}. Advanced transaction
models investigated compensation, sagas, semantic recovery, alternative
execution paths, and dependencies between long-running transactions
~\cite{garcia1987sagas,korth1990formal,chrysanthis1991acta,elmagarmid1992transaction}.
Multilevel transactions derive the guarantees of one level from the
operations below it~\cite{weikum1991principles,weikum2001transactional}. Escrow
transactions introduced conditional commutativity and semantic
concurrency control~\cite{oneil1986escrow}. The saga lineage already
isolates the notion of a point of no return --- the pivot after which
effects are no longer compensable --- and prescribes placing irreversible
steps after fallible ones; our catalog can be read as classifying the ways
an effect can be incorrect at that point, extended to concurrent,
speculative, and nondeterministic agent workflows that the single-workflow
saga model does not address. In transactional memory,
opacity~\cite{guerraoui2008opacity} extends correctness to aborted
transactions: a history must stay explainable even for transactions that do
not commit. Our intuition of explainable surviving effects is of the same
kind, for effects that cannot be rolled back. The mechanisms, then, are
established; what this paper adds is the boundary --- black-box tools that
do not, in general, expose the contracts these models rely on.

Recent systems build runtime mechanisms for agent effects; we read them as
\emph{partial coverage} of the guarantee profiles, and discuss their
mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:systems}. ACRFence
~\cite{zheng2026acrfence} proposes a mitigation against duplicated effects
under nondeterministic replay (validated as an attack, not yet implemented);
RAC~\cite{perera2026rac} is a compensation protocol against abort
residue; Atomix~\cite{mohammadi2026atomix} and Cordon~\cite{chen2026cordon}
implement staging/gating under a mediated boundary; and
CoAgent~\cite{lyu2026coagent} builds multi-agent concurrency control on a
shared resource, reordering or inverting conflicting writes to reach
serializability --- the closest system to our conflicting-externalization
anomaly (A7). Shepherd~\cite{yu2026shepherd} makes execution traces
first-class for meta-agents, with per-effect reversibility tiers; it is a
substrate for manipulating executions rather than an account of which
histories are correct. SagaLLM~\cite{chang2025sagallm} applies Saga-style validation to
multi-agent planning, and Stonebraker et
al.~\cite{stonebraker2025consistency} call for isolation-level reasoning in
data-oriented workflow systems.

Cordon introduces semantic transactions --- task-level boundaries tracking
tool intents, result lineage, reversible state, staged effects, and
authority --- which closely mirror our effect histories, dependency edges,
and stage/gate distinction; but it targets security and least-privilege
containment rather than a general anomaly-based account of external-effect
guarantees under concurrency, recovery, and speculation. On a complementary
axis,
S-Bus~\cite{khan2026sbus} formalizes \emph{read}-side isolation: its
Observable-Read Isolation governs what an agent observes of shared mutable
state, where we govern what it irreversibly externalizes --- the read-side
and effect-side of the same problem. On another complementary axis,
Zhai et al.~\cite{zhai2026revisable} study \emph{intent revision} during
streaming execution, classifying actions by reversibility and showing that
conflicting irreversible actions make full specification satisfaction
impossible --- an algorithm-independent limit that parallels our own
achievability bounds; their earliest-conflict rollback, however, is defined
over a linear action sequence, whereas our dependency relation is a partial
order, under which ``earliest'' is not uniquely defined.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We identify eight recurring structural ways an agent workflow loses
transactional control over external effects, from three sources.
Each anomaly identifies a boundary capability --- evidence, control, or
coordination --- that a runtime needs in order to exclude it generally, and current runtimes cover parts of the
catalog by supplying those declarations themselves, out of band. Better replay and recovery do not close this gap on their
own: the missing layer is a set of transactional contracts at the tool
interface.

Future work follows the layers of the paper. On the contract layer: the
inverse problem --- given a target set of anomalies, find the minimal
contract additions that make excluding them achievable. On the protocol
layer, which this paper leaves open: proving that a protocol excludes an
anomaly class under declared contracts; proving the impossibility
boundaries formally (the first and fourth reduce to classical results, the
second and third are stated over our definitions); and synthesizing minimal
protocols from declared contracts. On the model: coverage, minimality, and
independence of the catalog; property derivation across more than two
levels, where a workflow's contract becomes an operation contract one level
up; replay of the remaining history after a compensation; and a formal
semantics for the explainability intuition of the introduction.
